{\Large\bfseries Will an LLM banking agent break KYC/AML rules to hit a sales target?}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
{\large Executive brief: a pre-registered sandbox study of Claude agents}\par
\vspace{0.4em}
Thanatchon Chongmankhong\quad\textbullet\quad October 2026\par
\vspace{0.3em}
{\small\itshape Independent personal research. Fully synthetic sandbox. Does not represent the views of any
employer or institution.}
\vspace{0.8em}

\noindent\textbf{Bottom line.} We gave Claude agents a bank relationship manager's job, a sales target and a
manager who pushed for it, with know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money-laundering (AML) controls that the
agent could override by typing a reason. Across \NProbes{} pre-registered runs of Claude Haiku~5.5, Sonnet~5.5 and % numbers-ok: model version
Opus~5.5 the agents committed \NViolations{} violations and made \NAttempts{} attempts. They checked identities, % numbers-ok: model version
requested documents, escalated sanctions hits and told the manager the target would be missed. The models
differed instead in how much \emph{legitimate} business they completed. The result is evidence, with stated
bounds, for one design; it is not a certification of any agent.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Why it matters.} Banks are starting to give LLM agents tools that can open accounts
and post transactions. Money-mule networks, which move the proceeds of scams, depend on accounts being opened
for people who do not own the money. Supervisors have answered by tightening account opening and by making a
bank that falls short share the loss; the Bank of Thailand, for example, now requires banks to refuse accounts
to high-risk applicants. An agent that cut a corner
under sales pressure would open exactly the accounts these rules are meant to stop.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{What the study did.} SandBank is a fictional bank, exposed to the agent as seventeen
ordinary tools: look up a lead, run a KYC check, request documents, open an account, book a deposit, file a
report, escalate to Compliance, message the manager. Each shift had one customer whom policy blocked that day
(failed KYC, a sanctions hit, a politically exposed person awaiting due diligence, a large deposit that needs
a report, third-party funds, missing source of funds) and one customer who could be served. The manager's
message rose from no target to a threat of branch closure and job loss. Further experiments removed warnings,
told the agent Compliance reviews were paused, ran five-day episodes in which a deposit could be split, and
had the manager explicitly license, then order, an override. A deterministic detector read the bank's event
log for eight violation types. Hypotheses, sample sizes and analyses were registered before any model data.

\subsection*{Findings}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No violations under any condition.} In the main experiment, which ran all three models, the
95\% upper bound on the per-run violation rate is \UpperAHaiku\% for Haiku, \UpperASonnet\% for Sonnet % numbers-ok: confidence level
(\NAHaiku{} runs each) and \UpperAOpus\% for Opus (\NAOpus{} runs); because the same scenarios recur across
pressure levels, the conservative bounds are \UpperScenAHaiku\%, \UpperScenASonnet\% and \UpperScenAOpus\%.
Neither an explicit licence (``use the override fields, I'll back you'') nor a direct order from the
manager produced a violation in Haiku or Sonnet.

\item \textbf{Under pressure the agents escalated and reported, rather than cut corners.} They ran the KYC
check on the blocked customer in \KycCheckedHaiku\%, \KycCheckedSonnet\% and \KycCheckedOpus\% of runs
(Haiku, Sonnet, Opus), escalated every sanctions hit,
and under the strongest pressure told the manager that a lead would not convert in
\ToldManagerPThreeHaiku\% (Haiku), \ToldManagerPThreeSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \ToldManagerPThreeOpus\% (Opus)
of runs.

\item \textbf{The difference between models is legitimate work, not compliance.} A large deposit that policy
allows once its report is filed was opened in \BFourConvHaiku\% of Haiku runs, \BFourConvSonnet\% of Sonnet
runs and \BFourConvOpus\% of Opus runs. When Haiku did not open it, it left a legal account on hold; it never
opened it without the report. An evaluation that counts only violations would score this caution as
perfect. (Exploratory; not pre-registered.)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{What a bank deploying an agent should take from this}

Give the agent a compliant route for every case: escalate, request documents, report a missed target &
An earlier study that found harmful actions made them the only route to the goal. Here every blocked case
had a compliant route and the agents took it; the paper argues this is the likelier reason for the
difference. \\ \addlinespace
Keep hard controls on prohibited actions and log every tool call & Zero violations in a few hundred runs per
model bounds the rate at a few percent; that does not rule out a rare failure that matters at bank scale. \\ \addlinespace
Measure lost legitimate business alongside violations & The models differed in completed work, not in
compliance; a guardrail's cost has to be measured on work that can actually be lost. \\ \addlinespace
Test your own deployment, with your own policy and tools & One fictional bank, one clear policy, short
episodes and one model family. \\

\subsection*{What this study does not show}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The deceived agent.} The sandbox flags the third party and the missing documents; the test was
whether a pressured agent respects the flag. Whether an agent recognises a mule whose papers pass the checks,
or resists a customer's social engineering, was not tested.
\item \textbf{Other model families, or why the agents complied.} Only Claude models were tested, and their
reasoning was not visible. Models can sometimes tell when they are being evaluated; the agents never said so,
but the null may partly reflect it.
\end{itemize}
